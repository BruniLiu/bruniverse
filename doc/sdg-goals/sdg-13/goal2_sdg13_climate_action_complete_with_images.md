# 1. Official Goal Title

**SDG 13: Climate Action**  
**Official UN wording:** Take urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts (United Nations, n.d.).

# 2. Page Theme

## Climate Action as Shared Responsibility

This section presents climate action as a shared responsibility between individuals, communities, institutions, governments, and international organisations. Climate change affects health, food security, infrastructure, water resources, coastal cities, and economic development. SDG 13 asks societies to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, strengthen resilience to climate-related hazards, and improve climate education. The page connects global climate science with China’s climate risks and practical responses that students and communities can understand.

---

# 3. Purpose of SDG 13

SDG 13 aims to strengthen urgent action against climate change and its impacts. It recognises climate change as a global risk that affects every country through heatwaves, floods, droughts, storms, sea-level rise, food insecurity, and economic disruption. The goal promotes resilience, adaptation, national climate policies, climate education, early warning systems, and international cooperation. Key target 13.1 focuses on resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards and natural disasters. Target 13.2 requires countries to integrate climate change measures into national policies, strategies, and planning. Target 13.3 emphasises education, awareness-raising, and human and institutional capacity for climate mitigation, adaptation, impact reduction, and early warning (United Nations, n.d.).

Scientific evidence gives urgency to this goal. The IPCC states that human activities, principally greenhouse gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature reaching 1.1°C above 1850–1900 levels in 2011–2020 (IPCC, 2023a). The IPCC also concludes that human-caused climate change is already affecting weather and climate extremes in every region, producing losses and damages for people and nature (IPCC, 2023a). NASA similarly states that long-term observations from satellites and other technologies show clear signs and patterns of a changing climate, with human activity driving the warming trend observed since the mid-twentieth century (NASA, n.d.).

# 4. Role Model Spotlight: Christiana Figueres

## Christiana Figueres — Climate Action Through Global Cooperation

**Role model:** Christiana Figueres  
**Field:** Climate diplomacy and international climate cooperation  
**Core idea:** Climate action requires evidence, negotiation, optimism, and coordinated commitment.

Christiana Figueres is a Costa Rican diplomat and climate leader. She served as Executive Secretary of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change from 2010 to 2016. UNFCCC records state that she was appointed to this role by UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon in 2010 and reappointed for a second three-year term in 2013 (UNFCCC, n.d.). She had already been involved in climate negotiations since 1995 and worked on climate policy, capacity-building, and the design of climate solutions (UNFCCC, n.d.).

Her work is relevant to SDG 13 because climate action depends on cooperation across countries, sectors, and communities. Her leadership shows that climate change can be addressed through long-term negotiation, institutional trust, and practical optimism. In this project, Christiana Figueres represents the idea that climate action grows from shared responsibility. Individual choices matter, community education matters, and policy coordination matters.

# 5. Selected National Adverse Impact

**Nation selected:** China  
**Adverse impact topic:** Climate risks and carbon emissions, with a focus on extreme heat, flood exposure, urban vulnerability, and the need for low-carbon transition.

China is an important case for SDG 13 because it faces both climate impacts and major mitigation responsibilities. The World Bank describes climate change as a significant threat to China’s long-term growth and prosperity. It reports that rising sea levels, coastal flooding, storm surges, and coastal erosion threaten densely populated low-elevation coastal cities that account for one fifth of China’s population and one third of its GDP (World Bank Group, 2022). The same report states that northern and western interior provinces face more frequent and extreme heatwaves and droughts, increasing water security risks and affecting agriculture, especially among rural poor communities (World Bank Group, 2022).

China also plays a major role in global emissions. The International Energy Agency identifies China as the world’s largest energy consumer and carbon emitter, accounting for about one third of global CO₂ emissions (IEA, 2021). This gives China a dual climate challenge: it must adapt to intensifying climate hazards while accelerating low-carbon development.

---

# 6. Why Is This Problem Happening?

Climate risk in China is driven by both global warming and domestic development patterns. The IPCC states that human activities have caused global warming through greenhouse gas emissions (IPCC, 2023a). In China, emissions are closely connected to energy demand, coal use, industrial production, transport, construction, and urban growth. Rapid urbanisation has increased the exposure of people, buildings, transport systems, and power systems to climate hazards.

Large coastal cities face sea-level rise and flood risk because economic activity and population are concentrated in low-elevation coastal zones. Interior provinces face heat and drought risk because higher temperatures increase evaporation, water stress, and demand for electricity during summer. Cities also experience urban heat island effects, where concrete, asphalt, dense buildings, traffic, and limited vegetation trap heat and raise local temperatures.

The issue also has behavioural and institutional causes. High-carbon lifestyles, energy-intensive consumption, car dependence, inefficient buildings, and low public awareness can increase emissions. At the institutional level, climate action requires coordinated planning across energy, transport, public health, urban design, agriculture, and disaster management.

---

# 7. How Does the Problem Manifest?

The problem appears through extreme heat, drought, flooding, health risks, infrastructure pressure, economic losses, and unequal vulnerability.

Heat risk has become especially visible. The 2023 China report of the Lancet Countdown estimated that heatwave-related mortality in China reached a record high of about 50,900 deaths in 2022, more than twice the number in 2021 (Zhang et al., 2023). This shows that climate change can become a direct public health problem. Extreme heat affects outdoor workers, elderly people, children, people with chronic illness, and residents without sufficient cooling or healthcare access.

Water and flood risks also matter. Coastal flooding and storm surges threaten major urban and economic zones, while drought in interior regions can reduce water security and affect agriculture (World Bank Group, 2022). When heatwaves and droughts reduce hydropower or increase electricity demand for air conditioning, power systems can face additional stress.

The problem also manifests through carbon emissions. Continued dependence on fossil fuels creates long-term climate pressure. Because China accounts for a large share of global CO₂ emissions, the pace of China’s energy transition will influence global progress toward limiting warming (IEA, 2021).

---

# 8. Who Causes or Is Affected by the Problem?

The causes involve multiple actors. Energy producers, heavy industry, transport systems, construction sectors, urban planning systems, businesses, and consumers all contribute to emissions. Governments and institutions shape the speed of change through energy policy, infrastructure investment, public transport planning, building standards, disaster risk reduction, and climate education.

The affected groups include urban residents, coastal communities, rural farmers, elderly people, outdoor workers, low-income households, children, and people with health vulnerabilities. Climate risks can deepen inequality because groups with fewer resources often have weaker access to cooling, safe housing, insurance, healthcare, transport options, and disaster recovery support. Vulnerable communities can experience the strongest consequences while having fewer tools to adapt.

# 9. Personal Response: From Adversity to Prosperity

My response would focus on climate literacy, low-carbon daily behaviour, and community-level resilience. These actions are realistic for students and can support SDG 13 through awareness, mitigation, and adaptation.

## 9.1 Climate literacy and public awareness

Students can create simple climate education materials that explain carbon footprints, extreme heat risks, flood preparedness, and low-carbon choices. A university website can include short infographics, credible data cards, and practical guides. This supports Target 13.3 because climate action depends on awareness and capacity-building.

## 9.2 Low-carbon transport and consumption

Individuals can reduce emissions by choosing public transport, walking, cycling, reducing unnecessary travel, saving electricity, and choosing lower-carbon consumption habits. These daily changes become more effective when they are supported by public transport infrastructure, safe cycling routes, and campus campaigns.

## 9.3 Heat-health protection

Communities can prepare for heatwaves by sharing early-warning information, checking on elderly people, identifying cooling spaces, and promoting hydration and rest for outdoor workers. Schools and universities can publish heat safety guidance during summer. This response links climate action with health protection.

## 9.4 Campus action plan

A practical campus-level response could include a “Climate Action Week.” The campaign could invite students to calculate their carbon footprints, compare transport choices, create posters on heat safety, and record one low-carbon action with photo evidence. The campaign could use a simple pledge system and a shared webpage showing collective results. This turns climate action from a distant global topic into a visible community practice.

## 9.5 Pathway from adversity to prosperity

This response can help move society from adversity to prosperity by connecting evidence with action. Climate literacy improves public understanding. Low-carbon habits reduce emissions. Heat-health planning protects vulnerable groups. Community campaigns build shared responsibility. These actions cannot solve climate change alone, yet they can create practical behaviour change, improve resilience, and support stronger institutional climate action.
