## 3. Purpose of SDG 2

SDG 2 aims to create a world where all people have reliable access to safe, nutritious, and sufficient food. The goal covers hunger, malnutrition, agricultural productivity, small-scale food producers, sustainable farming, genetic diversity, and stable food markets. Target 2.1 calls for ending hunger and ensuring year-round access to safe, nutritious, and sufficient food for all people, especially poor and vulnerable groups. Target 2.2 focuses on ending all forms of malnutrition, including child stunting and wasting, and addressing the nutritional needs of adolescent girls, pregnant women, lactating women, and older people. Target 2.4 asks countries to make food production systems more sustainable, resilient, and adaptive to climate change and other environmental pressures (United Nations, n.d.).

The goal matters because food systems connect health, poverty reduction, climate resilience, land use, and social stability. A society can produce large amounts of food and still face nutrition problems, waste problems, or unequal access problems. SDG 2 therefore asks countries to think about the entire food system: production, distribution, consumption, nutrition, and sustainability.

**Field:** Hybrid rice, agricultural innovation, food security  
**Core idea:** Science can turn food scarcity into food security.

Yuan Longping is one of the most important figures in modern agricultural science. The World Food Prize Foundation describes him as the “Father of Hybrid Rice” and explains that he developed the technologies needed to breed the first hybrid rice varieties. His work helped improve food availability in China and supported broader global food security efforts. According to the World Food Prize profile, hybrid rice spread widely in China, and the higher-yielding crop helped nourish approximately 70 million more people per year in China alone (World Food Prize Foundation, n.d.).

Yuan Longping connects strongly to SDG 2 because his work shows how scientific research can address hunger and food insecurity at population scale. His contribution also shows that food security requires long-term investment, field experimentation, farmer adoption, and public support for agricultural innovation. For this project, he provides a role model for linking food systems with science, resilience, and social responsibility.

## 6. Selected National Adverse Impact

**Nation selected:** China  
**Adverse impact topic:** Food waste and sustainable food security pressure.

China has achieved major progress in food production and food security. The country feeds a large population with limited arable land and has invested heavily in grain production, agricultural technology, and food security governance. However, food loss and waste remain a serious challenge. IFAD reports that more than 35 million tonnes of food are lost or wasted in China annually, enough to feed 30 to 50 million people, with about 17 to 18 million tonnes wasted at the retail or consumption stage (IFAD, 2020). This issue connects directly to SDG 2 because food waste reduces the efficiency of food systems and places unnecessary pressure on land, water, energy, labour, and agricultural production.

Food waste also creates an ethical concern. When edible food is discarded, the resources used to produce, transport, store, and cook that food are lost as well. In a country focused on food security and agricultural resilience, reducing food waste can support a more sustainable food system and strengthen public awareness of responsible consumption.

---

## 7. Why Is This Problem Happening?

Food waste in China has several causes. First, rapid urbanisation and income growth have changed consumption habits. More people eat outside the home, order delivery, and participate in social dining. Restaurants, banquets, cafeterias, and takeaway services can produce waste when portion sizes are too large, ordering is excessive, or leftovers are not packed.

Second, cultural habits can increase waste in some dining situations. In group meals, hosts may order more food than needed to show generosity or hospitality. Feng, Marek, and Tosun (2022) discuss how cultural practices, including face-saving behaviour, can make restaurant food waste difficult to reduce through voluntary action alone.

Third, food loss occurs before food reaches consumers. Some food can be lost during harvesting, storage, transportation, processing, and distribution. Poor storage, long transport distances, cold-chain gaps, and market standards for appearance can all reduce how much edible food reaches the table.

Fourth, consumer awareness and measurement remain important barriers. People may underestimate how much food they waste. Without visible measurement, household planning, and clear feedback, food waste can remain hidden in everyday life.

---

## 8. How Does the Problem Manifest?

The problem appears through wasted meals, discarded leftovers, spoiled ingredients, over-ordering, and avoidable waste in restaurants, schools, households, and food service businesses. At the global level, UNEP estimates that 1.052 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022 at the retail, food service, and household levels, with households producing 631 million tonnes (UNEP, 2024). This global pattern helps explain why individual behaviour, school canteens, restaurants, and community campaigns are relevant to food security.

In China, food waste appears especially clearly in dining settings. IFAD reports that 17 to 18 million tonnes of food are wasted annually at the retail or consumption stage in China (IFAD, 2020). Feng et al. (2022) also note that food waste in major cities became a target of policy action, including the Clean Plate campaign and the 2021 Anti-Food Waste Law.

The issue has environmental consequences. Producing wasted food uses farmland, freshwater, fertilisers, energy, transport, labour, and packaging. When food is thrown away, these embedded resources are also wasted. Food waste can also increase greenhouse gas emissions when organic waste decomposes in landfills. These effects connect SDG 2 with SDG 12 on responsible consumption and SDG 13 on climate action.

---

## 9. Who Is Affected?

Several groups are affected by food waste and food security pressure.

**Consumers** are affected because wasted food increases household spending and reduces awareness of responsible consumption. Students and young people are especially relevant because dining habits, takeaway use, and canteen behaviour can shape long-term consumption patterns.

**Farmers and food producers** are affected because wasted food reduces the social value of their labour and can weaken the efficiency of the food system. When food is discarded after production, the effort and resources used in farming lose part of their impact.

**Restaurants, canteens, and retailers** are affected because food waste raises operating costs and creates reputational and legal pressure. China’s Anti-Food Waste Law aims to prevent food waste, safeguard national food security, conserve resources, protect the environment, and promote sustainable development (Standing Committee of the National People’s Congress, 2021).

**Low-income groups and vulnerable communities** are affected because food system inefficiency can contribute to higher costs and weaker access to affordable nutritious food. Reducing avoidable waste can support food redistribution, community food support, and better resource use.

**Future generations** are affected because food waste places pressure on land, water, energy, and climate systems. A sustainable food system protects long-term food availability and nutrition security.

## 10. Personal Response: From Adversity to Prosperity

A practical response should focus on reducing food waste at the level of daily behaviour, school communities, and small institutions. My response has three steps: measure, reduce, and share.

**Step 1: Measure food waste.**  
I would start by recording my own food waste for one week. This could include leftover rice, vegetables, snacks, takeaway food, and ingredients that expire before being eaten. A simple phone note or spreadsheet can record the food type, estimated amount, reason for waste, and possible prevention method. Measurement makes the problem visible and helps turn a vague concern into an actionable habit.

**Step 2: Reduce waste through meal planning and portion control.**  
I would choose smaller portions when ordering food, avoid excessive dishes in group meals, pack leftovers when possible, and plan meals before buying ingredients. In a campus setting, students can use a “small portion first, refill if needed” approach in canteens. Restaurants and cafeterias can offer half portions, reminder signs, and clear takeaway options.

**Step 3: Share awareness through a student-facing campaign.**  
A school or university campaign could connect SDG 2 with daily meals. The campaign could use posters, short videos, canteen data boards, and student pledges. The message should be simple: wasting less food supports food security, saves money, respects farmers, and reduces pressure on the environment.

These steps can help move from adversity to prosperity because they connect individual action with food system responsibility. Reducing food waste will not solve all food security challenges alone. It can still make food systems more efficient, build student awareness, and support a culture of respecting food. When more people measure and reduce waste, small daily actions can become a visible community norm.
