## 2. Page Theme

## Justice in the Digital Age: Strong Institutions Protect People Online and Offline

SDG 16 is often linked with courts, public security, legal systems, and peacebuilding. In a digital society, justice also includes protection from online harm, fraud, identity misuse, and organised digital crime. Telecom and online fraud can damage property, mental well-being, trust in digital services, and confidence in public institutions. This section explores how strong institutions, legal awareness, and public education can help people seek protection and justice in the digital age.

---

## 3. Purpose of SDG 16

SDG 16 aims to create peaceful, inclusive, and safe societies where people can live free from violence, access justice, and trust public institutions. It connects sustainable development with rule of law, accountability, transparency, legal identity, public access to information, and inclusive decision-making (UN DESA, n.d.). This goal matters because economic and social development relies on people feeling safe, protected, and fairly treated by institutions.

Several SDG 16 targets are especially relevant to this project. **Target 16.3** promotes the rule of law and equal access to justice for all. **Target 16.4** calls for reducing illicit financial flows and combating organised crime. **Target 16.6** focuses on effective, accountable, and transparent institutions. These targets connect directly with telecom and online fraud because fraud is a form of organised digital crime that requires legal enforcement, prevention, public education, and accessible reporting systems (UN DESA, n.d.).

Nadia Murad is a Yazidi human rights advocate and co-recipient of the 2018 Nobel Peace Prize. Her story connects strongly with SDG 16 because it shows that peace and justice require more than punishment after harm. People also need safe reporting channels, survivor-centred support, public recognition, and institutions that respond to violence with dignity and accountability. For this project, Nadia Murad represents the human side of strong institutions: laws and agencies should protect real people, especially people with less power to defend themselves.

## 5. Selected National Adverse Impact

## Telecom and Online Fraud in China

**Nation selected:** China  
**Adverse impact topic:** Telecom and online fraud as a challenge to digital safety, access to justice, and public trust.

Telecom and online fraud refers to fraudulently obtaining public or private property through telecom and network technologies, often through remote and non-contact methods (Ministry of Justice of the People’s Republic of China, 2023). This type of crime can involve fake customer service calls, investment scams, online shopping scams, loan scams, impersonation of authorities, phishing links, romance scams, and job-related scams.

China has treated telecom and online fraud as a major public security issue. The Supreme People’s Procuratorate reported that 78,000 people were prosecuted for telecom fraud crimes in 2024, a 53.9% increase from the previous year. Courts nationwide concluded 40,000 telecom fraud cases in 2024, involving 82,000 people (Supreme People’s Procuratorate of the People’s Republic of China, 2025). The Ministry of Public Security also reported that public security organs resolved nearly 1.95 million telecom and online fraud cases over a five-year period (Ministry of Public Security of the People’s Republic of China, 2024).

This issue is highly relevant to SDG 16 because it involves organised crime, protection of rights, institutional accountability, and equal access to justice. Victims need reliable institutions, clear reporting mechanisms, legal support, and practical prevention education.

## 6. Why Is This Problem Happening?

Telecom and online fraud is expanding because daily life has become increasingly digital. People use mobile payment, online shopping, social media, short-video platforms, online banking, delivery apps, job platforms, and digital identity systems. These services create convenience, and they also increase opportunities for criminals to contact potential victims remotely.

Fraudsters often exploit information gaps and emotional pressure. They may pretend to be police officers, bank staff, platform customer service workers, employers, investment advisers, or romantic partners. They use urgency, fear, greed, trust, shame, and authority to make victims act quickly. This makes fraud difficult to prevent through punishment alone. Prevention also requires legal literacy, psychological awareness, platform governance, and public education.

Young people are a relevant risk group. Lin, Wu, Sun, and Qu (2025) analysed survey data from 1,037 Chinese college students and found that routine telecom and cyber activities predicted higher odds of being targeted for telecom or cyber fraud. Qu, Lin, Wu, and Sun (2024) also found that about half of surveyed university students reported fear of cyber fraud during the past three months, while only 10% believed they were likely to experience cyber fraud victimisation in the next 12 months. This gap suggests that students may feel worried about fraud while still underestimating their own exposure to future risk.

## 7. How Does the Problem Manifest?

![Phishing and online fraud illustration](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Scam-phishing-fraud-email-attack-mail-online-system-cybercrime-information-access-credit-money-hack-hacker-laptop-malware-password-protection-software-steal-text-graphic-design-illustration-Material-property-techno.jpg)

Telecom and online fraud manifests through direct financial loss, identity misuse, psychological stress, and reduced trust in digital services. Victims may lose savings, tuition funds, family money, or borrowed money. Some victims may also experience shame, anxiety, self-blame, and fear of telling family members or authorities.

The problem also creates broader institutional pressure. Police, prosecutors, courts, banks, telecom companies, and online platforms must coordinate to freeze suspicious accounts, stop fraudulent communication, investigate cross-border crime, educate users, and support victims. The rise in prosecutions and court cases shows that law enforcement is active, and the continuing scale of cases shows that prevention and public legal awareness remain essential (Supreme People’s Procuratorate of the People’s Republic of China, 2025).

For students, common fraud scenarios include fake part-time jobs, fake online loans, fake customer service refunds, impersonation of teachers or school staff, fake shopping transactions, and investment-related scams. These scenarios often target students’ desire for income, convenience, independence, and quick problem-solving.

## 8. Who Is Affected?

The affected groups include students, elderly people, low-income workers, online shoppers, job seekers, small business owners, and people with limited digital literacy. Students are especially important in this project because they often use online platforms intensively and may lack experience with financial risk, contracts, personal information protection, and legal reporting procedures.

Families are also affected. A student’s financial loss can become a family burden, and emotional stress can spread to parents or caregivers. Institutions are affected as well. Universities may need to provide anti-fraud education, counselling, reporting guidance, and digital safety training. Banks, telecom operators, and platform companies must improve risk detection and cooperate with public authorities.

## 10. Personal Response: From Adversity to Prosperity

My personal response focuses on building a practical anti-fraud awareness toolkit for university students. The aim is to help students recognise fraud patterns before they become victims and to make help-seeking easier after suspicious contact occurs.

First, I would create a short “fraud scenario card” page for students. Each card would describe one common scam type, such as fake part-time job fraud, fake customer service refund fraud, fake loan fraud, impersonation fraud, and phishing link fraud. Each card would include warning signs, safe responses, and a simple reporting route.

Second, I would organise a peer-led digital safety session. Students often pay more attention to examples that feel close to their daily life. A peer-led session can use realistic screenshots, role-play dialogues, and short quizzes to show how scammers create urgency and pressure. The session should explain that smart people can still be deceived under emotional pressure. This helps reduce shame and encourages victims to seek help quickly.

Third, I would design a “pause before transfer” checklist. Before sending money, sharing verification codes, clicking links, or downloading remote-control apps, students could check four questions: Who is asking? Why is it urgent? What evidence do I have? Which official channel can verify it? This small habit can slow down impulsive decisions and reduce risk.

Fourth, I would connect the campaign with institutional support. A university webpage or campus poster should include the official anti-fraud app or hotline, campus security office contact, counselling service, and basic legal guidance. This makes the response more credible because students know where to go when something happens.

This response can help move society from adversity to prosperity because it turns fear into practical awareness. When students learn how fraud works, they become more confident digital citizens. When institutions provide clear reporting and support systems, victims can seek help faster. When communities discuss fraud openly, shame decreases and prevention becomes collective.

## 11. Credible Solution Paragraph

A student-focused anti-fraud awareness toolkit is a credible solution because cyber fraud prevention depends on both institutions and individual decision-making. Official enforcement can punish offenders, while public education can reduce victimisation risk before the crime succeeds. Research on Chinese college students shows that perceived guardianship, risky online behaviour, fear, and perceived risk are related to telecom and cyber fraud victimisation (Lin et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2024). A toolkit with realistic scenarios, verification habits, and reporting information can strengthen daily guardianship at the student level. This solution is realistic for a university project because it can be implemented through a website page, classroom presentation, posters, and peer education.
